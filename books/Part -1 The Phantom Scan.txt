"The world wants to be scanned. The machines want it, the governments want it,
 the corporations want it. They want every corner mapped, every person located,
 every secret distance reduced to a coordinate.

 What they don't expect is that children will understand this first.
 Children look at a game and see it for what it is.
 Sometimes that seeing kills them."

                                         — Ayan Bose, field notebook
                                           Kolkata, 2067

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────


P R O L O G U E

THE GREEN LINES


The monsoon had turned Chandannagar into something between a city and a river.
Water stood in every street, reflecting the low grey sky, and the air itself
had the quality of being liquidized — not just humid but actively transforming
into something else, something that hung between water and air and required you
to choose which element you were willing to submit to when you moved through it.

Ayan Bose was ten years old and he had noticed, with the specific quality of
attention that would define his adult life, that the green lines on Bilu's
modified mobile screen did not match the city as it actually existed.

The screen showed streets. The city had the same streets. But the green lines on
the screen were cleaner — tidier, more precise, ordered according to some
principle that the actual city had long ago abandoned in favour of monsoons and
makeshift repairs and the accumulated mud of generations. On the screen, the
Villa de l'Ermitage was clearly marked, its colonial walls rendered in perfect
3D, its courtyards and cellars decomposed into traversable digital space.

In the actual city, the Villa was simply abandoned — yellow walls going grey
with moss, windows sealed with plywood, the gate to its grounds hanging open
because the hinges had rusted through and nobody had bothered to fix them.

"It's worth seven hundred fifty credits," Bilu said, squinting at the screen.
The handset was old and heavily modified — a cracked piece of salvaged tech
with copper wire antenna rig he'd fashioned himself, wrapped around it like
vines. "Just for walking through it. The scan algorithm pays on density. More
square metres scanned, more credits. But the bigger bonus is for interior
volume. If I can get into the cellars, the bonus goes up to two thousand."

They were sitting on the flat roof of an old jute warehouse, their feet dangling
over the edge, the monsoon rain pattering around them in a pattern that seemed
almost coded — deliberate, rhythmic, designed. Below them, Chandannagar moved
through its afternoon in the specific dazed quality of monsoon-time, everything
slowed, everything accepting that the world was wet and would remain so and there
was no point in moving quickly.

Ayan did not have a phone. His father — Inspector Bose, honest man, scholar of
Tagore — had rules about screens and children, and they were firm. But he had
come to accept that his son would spend his summers with Bilu and Bablu and
Bachhu and Bhonbol, the loose squad of children who had appointed themselves
keepers of Chandannagar's mysteries, and he had learned not to ask too many
questions about what they did, because the answers were always the same:
nothing dangerous, just looking.

Just looking at things.

"That's not a game," Ayan said.

"Of course it's a game." Bilu rotated the screen, and the 3D model of the Villa
turned with it, its cellars visible now as a kind of cross-section, little rooms
rendered in the same clinical precision as the courtyards above. "Chasing
Phantoms. You collect the currency, you unlock achievements, you climb the
leaderboard. My score is in the top fifty in all of Bengal. If I can nail this
Villa scan, I'll be top twenty."

"The green lines," Ayan said.

"What about them?"

"They know where everything is. They're not a game. The games sit on top of
them, but the lines are something else."

Bilu looked at his friend — actually looked at him, the way he did when Ayan had
said something that required a shift in what Bilu was thinking — and then he
shrugged. "Sure. Fine. It's a game that sits on top of something else. Same
thing everyone's game does. The difference is this one pays." He looked back at
the screen. "And the Villa's been closed for thirty years. Why would anyone care
about the scan? It's just a dead building."

This was the flaw in Bilu's reasoning, Ayan would understand later. The belief
that nobody would care about something made it safe. The belief that something
was dead — abandoned, sealed, beyond function — made it useless to defend. And
the belief that a building was only a building, that its cellars were only
cellars, that the digital map was only a game.

The belief that the green lines were not also something else.

But Ayan was ten, and Bilu was eleven, and the monsoon had made everything seem
permissible — the water, the grey light, the sense that the normal rules were
temporarily suspended by weather. So when Bilu said "let's try it," Ayan said
"all right," and they went downstairs to find Bablu, and told the story that
would, over the course of a single afternoon, end with a child dead and with Ayan
learning something about the physical world that would take him thirty-five years
to fully understand.

CHAPTER    O N E

THE CHANDANNAGAR MYSTERY CLUB


The club had no official meeting place, which was one of the things that made it
a club — the understanding that the most important things existed in the gaps
between official places, in the spaces that nobody had claimed. Their
headquarters, by default, was the rooftop of the old jute warehouse because it
was high enough to see most of Chandannagar, including the rivers, and because
nobody important owned it anymore, and because Bablu had declared it so, and
what Bablu declared, the squad accepted.

Bablu was twelve and had appointed himself their leader because he had read
stories about Bengal's independence fighters — accounts of Keshab Chandra Sen and
Subhas Chandra Bose and all the young men who had believed that courage and
clear thinking could change the shape of the world. He carried a linseed-oiled
cricket bat everywhere, a symbol of defense, and he spoke in a way that suggested
he had considered each word and found it acceptable before allowing it to leave
his mouth. The other children deferred to him not because they were afraid of him
— they were not — but because Bablu had the specific quality of someone who had
decided something and would not change his mind, and this is the quality that
often passes for leadership in the worlds of children.

Bachhu was eleven, a girl who had the quality of moving through space the way
some people moved through water — efficiently, without wasted motion, with the
specific grace of someone who had learned early that her body was an instrument
for getting things done. She was fearless in the way that children can be fearless
when they have decided that fear is not a useful emotion and have not yet
encountered something that would teach them otherwise.

Bhonbol was twelve, broad-shouldered, with the soft quality of someone whose
strength was not yet hardened into something that required management. He had a
gentle way of talking and a habit of showing up with food — paper cones of roasted
peanuts, or milk sweets from the shop on Naskar Road — because he believed that
thinking worked better when the body was attended to. Later, when the violence
came, Bhonbol would be the one who held Panchu as the dog screamed, and this act
of holding something in pain would change him permanently.

Bilu was the technical engine, the one who understood how systems worked because
he had a mind that seemed calibrated for the space between physical things and
their digital representations. He carried his modified phone everywhere and he
talked about the networks the way other children talked about weather — as though
they were simply the conditions under which things happened, not things designed
by people with specific intentions.

Panchu had arrived one monsoon season, a scruffy, three-quarter-starved street dog
who had attached himself to the squad with the conviction of someone who believed
they were missing something in their composition. He had rough fur the colour of
wet clay and a sensitivity to high-frequency sounds — he would alert at the whine
of a drone passing overhead long before the children could hear it. Panchu's nose
was a organ of pure information — he could track a scent through rain, through
crowds, through the interference of a dozen other smells, and arrive at its source
with the certainty of someone following a map.

And there was Ayan Bose, who did not speak much and who carried a black notebook
that his father had given him for his tenth birthday, back in April, before the
monsoon had fully committed to transforming the city. His father had written on
the first page: *Write what you see. Not what you think about it. Just what you
see.* And Ayan had taken this instruction seriously in the way that children take
their parents' instructions seriously — as absolutes, as commandments, as the way
things ought to be done.

The Chandannagar Mystery Club had appointed itself guardian of a city that did not
particularly need guarding. They investigated the abandoned factories along the
Hooghly River. They tracked the patterns of the early morning cargo deliveries.
They had a detailed map of which shopkeepers watered their milk and which ones
gave honest measure. They had spent the previous monsoon tracking a series of
thefts from the local textile workshop and had successfully identified the thief
as a young man named Rajesh who had taken the fabric to pay for his mother's
medical care. They had not reported him. They had instead left an anonymous note
for the workshop owner suggesting that perhaps the missing fabric was something
the workshop could afford to lose.

This was the philosophy of the club: that the truth mattered, and that what you
did with the truth after you found it was separate from the finding.

On the afternoon that would become the last afternoon, they were assembled on the
roof with the monsoon pressing down from above and the city dissolving into grey
below. Bilu was showing them the Villa de l'Ermitage scan, rotating the 3D model,
explaining the credit structure, the bonus mechanisms, the way the game actually
worked — which was not like a game at all but like a system for converting the
physical world into digital information, converting the information into currency,
converting the currency into power.

"The thing is," he was saying, "right now I only have the external perimeter.
Walls, courtyards, the gate structure. But if I can get the interior — especially
if I can get the basement levels — the algorithm pays a massive bonus because
underground space is harder to scan with the drones. So the crowdsourced scans are
more valuable."

"Who pays?" Ayan asked.

Bilu looked at him. "I don't know exactly. The company that owns the game is
Niantic. But Niantic doesn't pay for the scans. They're paid by someone else, I
think. Someone who wants the maps."

"Maps of what?" Bablu said. "Maps of abandoned buildings? Why would anyone want
that?"

"Maps of everything," Bilu said. He looked at his screen. The green lines. The
perfect 3D rendering of the Villa's layout. "Think about it. Thirty years ago you
couldn't see anything without flying over it. Now, with the phones, with the
game, with kids like me walking through places with the scan running — the entire
world is getting mapped. Every corner. Every basement. Every place that humans can
fit themselves into."

"But it's just a building," Bachhu said.

"Today it's just a building. Tomorrow it's a thousand buildings. All of them
mapped. All of them available." Bilu paused. "My uncle works at the phone
network. He says the new military satellites are going to be able to see
everything. They're going to be able to watch any place, anywhere. But the
drones — the drones are going to need to know the ground. They're going to need
to have the terrain mapped at the resolution the game is creating."

None of them understood this completely, and Bilu was already moving on to the
specific mechanics of the Villa scan — the entry points, the probable layout, the
bonus structure — but Ayan wrote it down, in his new notebook, on a page with
the heading: *Green Lines.*

He wrote: *The game maps the world. The drones need the maps. The game is not a
game?*

And below that: *Bilu believes the green lines are the real thing. The game sits
on top of them.*

And below that: *Does this matter?*

CHAPTER    T W O

THE VILLA DE L'ERMITAGE


The Villa had been built in 1847, when the world still believed that Bengal could
be permanently held by people from outside of it. It was a colonial mansion,
designed to be imposing and successful and to broadcast, through every cream-
coloured stone and wrought-iron balcony, that the people inside it owned things
and would continue to own them because ownership was a permanent condition rather
than a temporary mercy.

This had proven incorrect.

By 1989, when the independence movements had fully resolved themselves and the
structures of colonial life had been dismantled or transformed, the Villa had
already begun its decline. It had passed through several owners, none of them able
to manage its expensive upkeep, and by 2010 it had been seized by the municipal
authority as a liability. By 2032, it had been sealed and abandoned so thoroughly
that most of Chandannagar had simply forgotten it existed.

Except the maps had not forgotten it. The game remembered every corner.

The gate was sealed, but the lock had been open for so long that the mechanism
had seized and become permanent, and so it opened not with a click but with a
grinding of rust and surrender. Inside, the courtyard was filled with the kind of
green that comes from thirty years of undisturbed growth — vines that had climbed
the walls with the certainty of something that knew it had won, moss that covered
the original stone so thoroughly you could not see the seams between the bricks.

It was beautiful in the way that decay is beautiful — honest, complete, without
apology.

Bilu led them, his modified phone out in front of him like a divining rod,
watching the 3D model, checking their position against the rendered courtyard.
Behind him, Bablu carried his cricket bat. Bachhu moved with the specific
carelessness of someone who does not expect consequences. Bhonbol and Ayan
followed, and Panchu moved with them, his three-legged gait adapted perfectly
now to the uneven ground, his nose working the air.

The monsoon had filled the lower courtyards with standing water, and they waded
through it, the water warm and dark and probably carrying things you did not want
to think about. Panchu did not mind the water. He moved through it like he was
born for it.

"The entrance is there," Bilu said, pointing. The main doors to the Villa proper
were sealed with plywood, but the plywood had warped over years of monsoons, and
there was a gap — not quite wide enough for a person, but Bachhu was small, and
she had the habit of slipping through spaces that seemed impossible. She squeezed
through, disappeared into darkness, and then the plywood shifted slightly and
there was an opening.

They moved into the Villa.

It was cold inside, genuinely cold, the kind of cold that stone buildings produce
when they have been sealed against sunlight and air circulation. The darkness was
almost complete, and Bilu had to use his phone's light to navigate, the bright LED
creating pools of visibility in a space that seemed actively resistant to seeing
anything. They moved deeper, and the smell changed — not bad, exactly, but
present, the smell of stone and stagnant water and the slow disintegration of
things that had been sealed away.

"The cellars are below the main hall," Bilu said, checking the rendered plan.
"There's a door somewhere on the eastern wing. It should be — there."

It was there. A heavy wooden door with iron hinges, warped by water damage but
still functional. Bablu pulled it open — the hinges groaning, water pooling below
— and the darkness underneath seemed even darker, as if the darkness of the Villa
proper had concentrated itself down here and was waiting.

They descended carefully, feeling their way along a stone wall, Bilu's phone light
tracking the path. The water got deeper. By the time they reached the bottom of
the stairs, it was mid-calf, warm, and absolutely opaque.

"The scan requires full coverage," Bilu was saying, working his way deeper. "The
algorithm is looking for volumetric density. More space covered, more credits. I'm
going to walk the perimeter, get the full layout registered. Should take maybe
ten minutes, then we leave."

The cellars were vast — a series of underground rooms that seemed to extend
indefinitely into darkness. Some had clearly been storage. Some had the layout of
small living quarters, suggesting that the Villa had once required staff quarters
underground, which is the way of colonial houses. Some were simply open space,
their purpose long since abandoned.

Ayan moved separately from the group, running his hand along the walls, noticing
things. The stone here was different — newer than the original Villa stone. The
brick pattern was irregular, as though it had been repaired and modified
repeatedly. And there were rust stains on the stone, the distinctive blood-colour
of oxidized iron, which meant metal had been exposed to water here for a very long
time.

He wrote, in his notebook, once they were back outside: *Weapons storage. The
cellars were weapons storage. The rust pattern suggests ammunition. The irregular
brickwork suggests repeated smuggling routes in and out.*

But he did not know this yet. He was ten, moving through dark water, watching his
friend with the modified phone, not understanding that the Villa was a place where
something was happening, or had happened, or was about to happen.

He was just looking.

CHAPTER    T H R E E

THE TERMINAL


Bilu moved confidently through the flooded cellars, the phone's light bobbing
ahead, the scan app running continuously. The algorithm was recording every
surface, every corner, building a three-dimensional map of the space with
millimetric precision. The app showed progress in the corner of the screen: 34%,
38%, 41%.

He was moving toward what appeared to be a sealed equipment locker, its door long
ago rusted shut, when the light caught something — a glint of metal below the
water, just visible. Bilu stopped.

"Wait," he said.

They waited. Bilu waded to the spot, his phone held carefully above water, and
bent down. When he straightened, he had something in his hand — a casing, a brass
cartridge, discolored with age but still maintaining its basic shape.

"Ammunition," he said. He looked at it with the careful analysis of someone who
had learned to identify things from being curious about how they were constructed.
"Old. Like, forty years old maybe. This place had guns."

"Put it down," Bablu said. Quietly.

But Bilu was already moving deeper, drawn forward by the specific gravity of
discovery, the pulling sensation that something was here that he was supposed to
find. The water was deeper now, up to his ribs, and the darkness seemed to press
more actively. And then the light caught something else — this time, not in the
water.

There was a room. Not a cellar room like the others, but something different.
Built into the brick, partially concealed behind a false wall panel, there was a
small enclosed space. And inside that space, bolted to a stand, was equipment.

A terminal. A security-grade digital terminal with keypad access, the kind used
for controlled entry systems. And beside it, in a weatherproofed case, was a data
relay system — an encrypted uplink node, the kind that would have been used to
manage remote facility operations.

It was active. The lights on the node were glowing red and green, the colors
shifting on a slow cycle. Still powered. Still connected.

"That's a Vantor system," Bilu said. His voice had changed. He was looking at the
terminal the way a person looks at something they have not expected to find and
do not immediately understand. "That's a military-grade Vantor command terminal.
What is that doing in a sealed, abandoned building?"

"We should leave," Bablu said. He was not a naturally fearful person, but he had
the specific instinct that certain discoveries were not discoveries you were
meant to make. "We should leave now."

But Bilu was already moving toward the equipment, drawn forward by the gravity of
the finding. He approached the terminal, his phone still held carefully, and he
reached out and touched the keyboard.

It responded. The screen, which had been dark, flickered to life, displaying a
command prompt, waiting for input. Bilu looked at his phone, at the scan data
still accumulating, and he made a decision.

He started typing commands, trying to access the underlying directory structure.
The scan was still running — 56%, 58%, 61% — and he was seeing something in the
data that interested him. The volumetric calculations, the precision of the spatial
mapping, the way the algorithm was breaking the cellar down into usable
coordinates.

"The scan algorithm," he said, without looking away from the screen, "it's the
same structure as military targeting data. Same coordinate system. Same volumetric
breakdown. These aren't games, Bablu. I don't think they ever were."

He pulled out a connecting cable from his pocket — he always carried cables, he
carried cables the way other boys carried nothing — and he plugged his phone into
the terminal.

The data transfer began instantly. Not from the phone to the terminal, but from
the terminal to the phone. Raw files, streaming directly into Bilu's device,
gigabytes of coordinate data, mapping information, tactical overlays. The scan
progress indicator disappeared and was replaced by a file transfer dialog:

*VILLA_DE_L_ERMITAGE_COMPLETE_SCAN_COPY.ZIP — 847 MB TRANSFERRED*

It was happening too fast to stop. Bilu was watching the transfer, his eyes wide,
and then a sound came from deeper in the cellars — the sound of footsteps in
water, heavy footsteps, the sound of someone moving with purpose toward them.

"We need to go," Bablu said. He was moving backward toward the stairway, his
cricket bat raised, not in aggression but in the defensive posture of someone who
understood that something had changed.

The footsteps got closer.

Bilu was still holding the phone, still waiting for the transfer to complete, and
he was not moving, and this was the moment in which everything became different.

The transfer completed. The dialog closed. The files were on Bilu's phone.

A man stepped into the cellar.

CHAPTER    F O U R

CASPAR RIEL


He was young — twenty-six years old, though he moved with the cold certainty of
someone much older — and he wore a dark, high-collar waterproof coat that had no
logos, no brand markers, no reflective safety stripes. His face was sharp and his
eyes were pale blue and entirely empty of expression, the way eyes are empty when
the person inside has decided that emotion is a luxury item that interferes with
work.

His name was Caspar Riel, though Ayan would not know this until much later. In
this moment, he was simply a man with a gun, appearing at the precise moment when
a child decided he needed to copy files from a classified military terminal to a
game application on his phone.

Riel's gaze moved through the flooded cellar, taking in the children, taking in
the terminal, taking in the cable still connected between the phone and the
Vantor system. He calculated the situation with the same precision the game
algorithm used to calculate space.

He did not shout. He did not panic. He did not treat the situation like it was
an emergency, because to Riel, all situations were the same — problems that
required solutions, and solutions that required efficiency.

He moved toward Bilu with a stride that was economical and complete. His hand
clamped around Bilu's throat. The boy's breath caught. His feet left the water-
covered floor, and he was slammed backward against the brick pillar that
supported the false wall.

"Hey!" Bablu screamed, the cricket bat rising. "Let him go!"

Bablu charged, moving with the strength and anger of a twelve-year-old who had
suddenly understood that something was genuinely wrong, that the game was not a
game, that the afternoon had crossed a boundary from which it could not return.

He swung the cricket bat with all his force, aiming for Riel's torso.

Riel raised his left forearm, catching the bat on the bone. The wood made contact
with a dull *thack*, but Riel's arm did not yield, did not break, did not show
any sign of feeling impact. He pivoted, using the contact as a fulcrum, and drove
a front-kick toward Bablu's chest.

The kick landed with the precision of someone who had practiced this motion a
thousand times. Bablu flew backward, his feet leaving the water, his body
slamming into the floor with an impact that drove all the breath from his lungs.

He came up gasping, his wrist making an angle it was not meant to make. Broken.
He knew it instantly. The pain was so complete it seemed to have color, and his
vision swam around the edges.

Riel had not looked away from Bilu the entire time.

"I... I didn't take the files," Bilu gasped, his voice strangled by the pressure
on his throat. "Please—"

Riel's pale blue eyes held no anger — only calculation, only the mathematical
assessment of a problem that required a specific solution. He reached into his
dark coat and withdrew a 9mm handgun with the comfortable familiarity of someone
who had done this before, who had drawn a gun in dark rooms and flooded cellars
and midnight streets with the frequency of practice.

Ayan watched from the shadows where he had stepped when Riel first appeared. He
wanted to scream but his throat was locked, his entire body in the grip of a fear
so pure it felt like it was changing his physical composition, turning his blood
into water, his bones into ice.

He saw the gun. He saw the tiny stamp on the brass casing of the chambered round:
*EU-31*. European manufacture. Military-grade ammunition.

Riel pressed the muzzle directly behind Bilu's left ear, angling it downward.

*"No!"* Bachhu shrieked from behind one of the pillars.

Riel pulled the trigger.

The sound in the enclosed brick cellar was not a sound so much as a physical force,
a deafening *CRACK* that shattered the ancient plaster of the ceiling and sent a
shower of dust falling into the dark water. In the moment of that sound, Ayan saw
his friend's face go slack. He saw Riel release his grip. He saw Bilu's small body
collapse sideways into the shallow, tea-coloured water, his face turned east,
toward the Hooghly River, toward Manchester, toward anywhere but here.

The blood pooled instantly. A dark blossom spreading through the silt. Ayan's
eyes recorded it with the specific precision that shock produces — every detail,
every angle, every colour rendered in perfect, terrible clarity.

"Run," Bhonbol whimpered. His voice cracked into a sob.

Riel turned his pale eyes toward the remaining darkness. He raised the gun.

"Run!" Bablu choked out, cradling his shattered wrist, dragging himself toward the
stairs with his feet.

The children scrambled upward, desperate, Bachhu sobbing hysterically, Bhonbol
scooping up Panchu, Bablu pulling himself up with one good arm. They burst toward
the stairway and as they reached the top of the stairs, Riel fired a single,
parting shot into the darkness.

The bullet struck the brick wall, showering them with red dust, and then
ricocheted downward. Panchu let out a horrific, high-pitched scream as the
projectile shattered his rear leg. The dog tumbled down the steps in a flurry of
blood and yelping, and Bhonbol scooped him up and they burst into the monsoon
rain, running through the muddy streets of Chandannagar until their lungs burned
and the yellow walls of the Villa de l'Ermitage had vanished behind the grey
curtain of water.

CHAPTER    F I V E

AFTER


They hid in an abandoned jute warehouse near the railway tracks, a structure so
old that its corrugated tin roof moved like paper in the monsoon wind. The rain
drummed overhead with the sound of a thousand iron hammers. Nobody spoke.

Bablu rocked back and forth, cradling his broken wrist, his face white with shock
and pain. Bhonbol sat in the corner holding Panchu, who had stopped screaming but
whose rear leg was at an angle that made Ayan's stomach tighten when he looked at
it. Bachhu stared blankly at the wet floor, her eyes wide, her hands shaking so
violently she had to sit on them.

Bilu's modified phone lay on the dirt floor between them, its screen flickering.
The *Chasing Phantoms* application was being wiped remotely — Ayan could see the
process, the files being deleted, the scan data being purged from the device. A
clean grey notification appeared on the screen:

    SYSTEM ERROR // NIANTIC SPATIAL
    File 'Villa_de_l_Ermitage_Scan' corrupted.
    Target location retired from database.
    Your account has been credited: 0.00

The digital world had simply deleted Bilu. Deleted the afternoon. Deleted all
evidence that the files had been copied or that any scan had taken place.

Ayan sat apart from the others, his hands covered in mud and his dead friend's
blood. The blood had splashed on his shirt when Riel pulled the trigger, and it
had dried now to a dull brown colour, indistinguishable from the mud of the
monsoon streets.

He reached into his plastic-wrapped pocket and withdrew his new black notebook —
the gift from his father for his tenth birthday, still clean, still unused — and
with shaking, blood-stained fingers, he carefully unwrapped it.

He opened it to the first page. The paper was clean, white, real. It did not
require a signal. It had no sensors. It could not be remotely wiped by servers in
Brussels or San Francisco or anywhere else. It simply was.

He took out his small graphite pencil. With careful, deliberate, shaking strokes,
he wrote his first entry:

    **July 20, 2032. Chandannagar.**
    
    The Case: The murder of Bilu.
    
    The Spot: Villa de l'Ermitage. Cellars. Beneath the false wall where the Vantor
    terminal sits.
    
    The Wound: Left occipital bone. Entry angle downward. Close range. Execution-
    style.
    
    The Weapon: 9mm handgun. Casing stamped EU-31. Military manufacture.
    
    The Killer: Young. Twenty-six maybe. Cold blue eyes. Dark coat with no logos.
    Carries no phone. Has a physical paper map instead. Moves with military precision.
    
    The Lesson: The green lines on the screen are not a game. The game sits on top
    of them, but underneath, the lines are a map. A map for the machines. A map for
    the drones. The screens are designed to lie. They hide what is underneath.
    
    Trust only what has physical weight.
    
    Trust only what cannot be remotely deleted.
    
    Trust only what you write down by hand.

He closed the notebook with a soft, final *snap*. The sound seemed to echo in the
warehouse, seemed to matter in a way that the digital deletion of Bilu's data did
not. A physical sound. A real sound.

His father would receive the broken wrist of Bablu, the stuttered trauma that
would change Bhonbol forever, the terrified withdrawal of Bachhu. He would receive
the three-legged dog that Ayan would beg to amputate in their kitchen, using the
surgical knowledge of his years on the police force. He would receive the phone
that they had not been able to wipe clean, and he would understand, immediately,
what had happened.

And he would launch his own investigation — a rogue investigation, thorough and
precise, into the weapons smuggling operation that the Vantor terminal in the
Villa's cellars had been part of. He would file reports. He would pursue evidence.
He would find that the supply lines ran up through the military-industrial complex,
that Vantor had been running an off-books weapons operation out of a sealed
colonial mansion in Bengal, that the system that had wiped Bilu's phone was the
same system that would later build the drones that would dominate warfare.

And for this investigation, for his refusal to accept the official "accidental
drowning" narrative, for his insistence on the physical evidence that nobody
wanted to see, Inspector Bose would be systematically dismantled. Demoted.
Stripped of his pension. Transferred to an archive desk where he would die a
broken, bitter man, leaving his son with nothing but a worn copy of Gitanjali
and a burning hatred for systems that protected themselves by destroying evidence.

But that was all after.

In the moment of the jute warehouse, with the monsoon pressing down and Bilu dead
and Panchu's leg hanging at an angle, there was just Ayan, aged ten, writing in
his notebook, learning the first true lesson: the physical world persists in
telling the truth even when the systems built on top of it have decided to do
otherwise.

The bullet angle. The blood pooling. The metallurgical signature of the round.
The broken bone of Bablu's wrist. The shattered leg of a three-legged dog.

These things do not require signals. They do not get remotely deleted. They simply
are.

And they wait.

E P I L O G U E

THIRTY-FIVE YEARS LATER


High above the rain-swept jute fields of Chandannagar, a red navigation light
blinked once through the monsoon clouds. A Vantor reconnaissance drone, smaller
and more elegant than the machines that would dominate the battlefields of 2067,
but built on the same principles, carrying the same mapping technology, navigating
terrain that had been scanned by children playing a game.

Down on the ground, in the dark of the abandoned warehouse, Ayan Bose held his
physical notebook in his hands — real, permanent, impossible to delete — and the
terrible, bloody arithmetic of his life had officially begun.

He would become an investigator. He would pursue physical evidence with a
devotion that bordered on religious. He would believe, with absolute conviction,
that the truth persists in the world even when systems decide it should be buried.
He would fail at his marriage, lose his custody hearing, and become the kind of
man who only functions when there is a case in front of him, because a case is
the one place where his method was vindicated.

And thirty-five years later, on a volcanic plateau in Iceland, he would find a
bullet behind the left ear of a twenty-nine-year-old man named Marcus Hale, and he
would understand, instantly and completely, that Riel had not stopped working in
the cellars of Chandannagar. That the green lines had not remained a game. That
the machines had grown larger and more efficient, and the men who ran them had
learned to do more terrible things, but the principle was the same.

The physical evidence survives.

The body keeps its truth.

He would sit in a storage-room office in Reykjavik-9, facing the rain, facing the
machines in the sky, facing the specific weight of a thirty-five-year-old crime
that had shaped everything he was, and he would write: *Body fell facing east.*

And it would all come clear.

The lines were not games. The drones needed maps. The maps came from children with
phones. The children who asked the wrong questions died in cellars, their deaths
officially erased, but the physical evidence — a bullet, a wound angle, a blood
pattern — persisted, demanded to be seen.

And a detective born in the monsoon of Chandannagar, who had learned at ten years
old that paper was the only honest medium, would spend his life assembling that
evidence until it became undeniable.

The dead always speak.

You just have to know how to listen.
